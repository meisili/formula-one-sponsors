§ 01 — WHY THIS STORY
Lede / intro (~150 words)
Every patch of carbon fiber on a Formula 1 car has a price. Running a top team costs upward of $200 million a year, and less than half of that comes from broadcasting and prize money. The gap is filled by sponsors — and the names on the cars are a public ledger of which industries decided global motorsport visibility was worth buying, and when.
For most of the sport's modern history, that ledger was dominated by a single category: tobacco. From the early 1980s through 2006, cigarette brands accounted for nearly a third of every title sponsorship in Formula 1. When the European Union banned tobacco advertising in motorsport, that revenue stream collapsed in a single season — and what replaced it tells you something about how global money has moved over the last twenty years.
This project tracks 45 years of F1 sponsorship across two datasets: every title sponsor since 1981, and every visible sponsor on every car for the last six seasons.
Ferrari livery captions
1999 — Ferrari F399 (single dominant sponsor)

Ferrari's 1999 livery was anchored by a single name above all others. Marlboro had been the team's title sponsor since 1997 and would remain so until 2010 — a 14-year contract that outlasted the EU advertising ban itself. Around it sat a small cluster of partners: Shell on the engine cover, Fiat in the corporate slot, a watch brand, a tire brand, an electronics brand. Six logos. The car looked like a billboard for one company.

2026 — Ferrari SF-26 (fragmented)

The 2026 SF-26 has no title sponsor at all. The team is Scuderia Ferrari HP — but HP is one of roughly 32 visible partners on the car, sharing space with IBM, Shell (still there after four decades), Ray-Ban, Velas, VGW, Santander, and a long tail of smaller logos. Where the 1999 car concentrated commercial attention, the 2026 car distributes it.

Reader education sidebar — "What a title sponsor is"

Throughout this story we distinguish between title sponsors — the company whose name appears in the team's official entry, the way "Stake F1 Team" or "Oracle Red Bull Racing" reads on the official grid — and the broader pool of partners whose logos appear on the car. Title sponsorship is contractually distinct, historically dominant, and easier to track over time. We use title sponsors for trend analysis going back to 1981, and the full sponsor roster for the recent grid.

Why 1981 sidebar

Formula 1 became a commercially structured sport in 1981. The Concorde Agreement that year formalized the relationship between teams, the FIA, and the commercial rights holder (then FOCA), making title sponsorship a stable contractual position for the first time. Before 1981, sponsorship existed but the structure around it didn't.

§ 02 — THE HISTORICAL TREND
Transition (~50 words)

The names that have appeared on F1 cars over 45 years cluster into a small number of industries — only ten of the twenty NAICS sectors used by the U.S. government to classify all business activity appear at all. The shape of the data tells a story in three acts.

Stacked bar chart caption

Each column represents one season. The height shows how many cars carried a title sponsor that year; the colors show what industry those sponsors came from. Tobacco — shown as the darkest band — was the single largest category for 25 years before disappearing almost entirely after 2006.

Three-era explanation

The tobacco era (1981–2006). Cigarette brands were the single largest category of F1 title sponsor for 25 consecutive years. Marlboro, Camel, Rothmans, John Player Special, Mild Seven, Gauloises, Gitanes, Lucky Strike, Benson & Hedges, West, and Winfield all held title slots, sometimes multiple at the same time. In 2000, six of the ten teams on the grid had a tobacco brand in their name. Title sponsorship in this era was effectively a tobacco market — one of the few channels left for the industry to reach a global audience.
The vacuum (2007–2016). The European Union's tobacco advertising directive took effect in 2005, and the FIA's voluntary phase-out completed by 2007. The collapse was nearly total: from six tobacco title sponsors in 2006 to one in 2007. Telecoms tried to fill the space — AT&T at Williams, Vodafone at McLaren, Orange at Arrows — and most exited within a few years. Industry diversity actually fell during this decade rather than rising; the data shows seven NAICS sectors represented in the 1990s versus only five in the 2010s.
The reinvention (2017–2026). The post-Liberty Media era is the first sustained expansion of F1 sponsorship since the tobacco era ended. Three categories now share what tobacco used to hold: software (Oracle, HP, Atlassian, Cognizant), oil and gas (Aramco, Petronas, Orlen) — much of it state-owned — and a wave of crypto and fintech (Visa Cash App, MoneyGram, Stake). Where tobacco was one industry buying global visibility, the current grid is many industries with overlapping motives.

Key stat block annotations

92 title sponsorships in the 1980s — the highest decade on record.
47 in the 2010s — nearly halved. The decline is structural, not industry turnover.
−49% over thirty years. Total F1 revenue grew across this period; what changed is how teams package their commercial inventory. One large title contract became thirty or more smaller principal/major/minor placements. Same money, distributed across more logos.

§ 03 — WHERE THE MONEY COMES FROM
Section intro (~100 words)

The industries that have funded Formula 1 have shifted, but the geography has shifted further. For most of the sport's commercial history, F1 was structurally European — Italian tobacco brands, French oil companies, German auto suppliers, British banks. That pattern began to fracture in the early 2000s as Asian manufacturers entered (Bridgestone, Petronas, Panasonic, Canon), and broke open again in the 2020s when state-owned Middle Eastern energy companies arrived. Today's grid is multi-polar in a way it has never been before — and the timing of who came in when is itself a story.

Three-wave explanation

Western European dominance (1981–late 1990s). Italian tobacco brands like Marlboro and Mild Seven (PMI's Italian-domiciled operations); French oil (Elf, Total, Gauloises, Gitanes); German auto suppliers (Braun, Mahle); British financial institutions (Barclay's, HSBC). Through the mid-1990s, Western Europe accounted for the overwhelming majority of title sponsorships on the grid.
Wave one — Asia Pacific (post-2000). Petronas joined Sauber in 2002. Japanese names that had been suppliers became title sponsors — Panasonic at Toyota, Canon at Williams, Bridgestone as a tire supplier with title-level visibility. Asia Pacific's share doubled within a decade.
Wave two — the Middle East (post-2018). Aramco became Aston Martin's title partner in 2022. Orlen — Polish, not Middle Eastern, but with similar state-energy logic — joined Alfa Romeo in 2020. ADNOC followed in 2023. Three state-owned energy companies took title positions in three consecutive years. The pattern is not coincidence: as the sport added races in Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Abu Dhabi, and Qatar, sovereign-wealth-adjacent sponsorship followed the calendar.

Chart caption

Each year shows the share of title sponsorships by the sponsor's headquarters region. Years with no valid title sponsors appear as zero. Western Europe's near-monopoly through the 1990s is visible as the dominant band; the multi-polar mix of the 2020s is what's new.

§ 04 — THE CURRENT GRID
Section intro (~120 words)

The 2026 grid is the most commercially saturated in Formula 1 history. Across all ten teams, there are 329 distinct sponsorship placements on cars and drivers' suits — counting title, principal, major, and minor partners. That number has grown roughly 15% since 2020. McLaren alone carries 55 logos; Cadillac, in its debut season, carries only nine.
What that saturation looks like geographically tells a story Liberty Media has been telling shareholders for nine years. In 2020, 26% of full-grid sponsors were headquartered in the United States. By 2026, that figure is 45%. Western Europe has dropped from majority to 40%. The Middle East accounts for only 3% of sponsor count — but those few sponsors are concentrated almost entirely at the title or principal tier, where the money is.

Map annotations

Concentration vs. count. The map shows where sponsors are headquartered. It does not show where the money is concentrated. Three Saudi-domiciled sponsors at the title tier represent more committed revenue than fifty US sponsors at the minor tier — sponsor count is not sponsor dollars.

Geographic snapshot key findings

US growth. The most consistent geographic shift across 2020-2026 is the rise of US-headquartered sponsors — from 26% to 45%, a 19-point swing in six years. That tracks directly with Liberty Media's stated strategic priority of expanding F1's American audience and adding US races (Miami 2022, Las Vegas 2023).
Western European hold. Despite the US surge, Western Europe remains the second-largest source of sponsors at 40%. The category is broader than it looks — the data includes UK financial services, Italian luxury goods, German engineering, and French aerospace.
Asia Pacific flat. 8% in 2026, essentially unchanged from 2020. Notable given that F1 races in Japan, Singapore, China, and Australia.
The China gap. China is underrepresented in the sponsor data relative to its presence on the race calendar. The Chinese Grand Prix returned to Shanghai in 2024, but Chinese corporate money has not followed Chinese consumer interest into the paddock at any scale. The closest is Zhou Guanyu's individual sponsors during his Sauber tenure — a driver-level pattern, not a commercial one.

§ 05 — CRYPTO IN THE PADDOCK
Section intro (~150 words)

The story of cryptocurrency in Formula 1 fits into 36 months. In 2020, the grid carried two crypto brands. By the start of 2022, that number was 13 — across nine of the ten teams. In November of that year, FTX filed for bankruptcy. Mercedes removed FTX branding from its cars mid-season and refused to return the unspent portion of a $30 million annual deal to the bankruptcy estate, citing contractual terms. By the start of 2023, the crypto count on the grid had dropped by nearly half.
What's striking is what happened next. Crypto did not exit Formula 1. By 2026, there are 11 active crypto sponsors on the grid — close to the 2022 peak, but the names have almost completely turned over. FTX, Vauld, Velas, Iqoniq, QNTMPAY, and Floki are gone. OKX, Binance, Bybit, Kraken, Gate, and Tezos remain or have arrived. The category survived; the specific gamble each team made on it didn't.

FTX annotation callout

November 2022 — FTX collapse. Sam Bankman-Fried's exchange files for bankruptcy after a liquidity crisis driven by Alameda Research's exposure to its own FTT token. Mercedes immediately covers FTX logos on the car for the final Brazilian and Abu Dhabi races. Mercedes had received a reported $30M upfront for the 2022 season; the bankruptcy estate later sought to claw back portions of that payment. The Mercedes deal is the largest single sponsorship collapse in modern F1 — the only comparable case in the dataset is Leyton House at March, after Akira Akagi's arrest in the Fuji Bank fraud scandal in 1991.

About this project / methodology section
I'd suggest adding a short methodology note at the bottom. Here's a draft:

About this project. Two datasets underpin the reporting. The title sponsor dataset covers every Formula 1 team entry from 1981 through 2026, with each entry classified by the title sponsor's industry using the 2022 North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) — the same taxonomy used by the U.S. Census Bureau and the federal H-1B visa program. The full sponsor dataset covers every visible sponsor on every car for the 2020 through 2026 seasons, sourced from team-released livery imagery, official team partner pages, and contemporaneous reporting.
NAICS codes were assigned by mapping each sponsor's primary business activity to the 2022 NAICS taxonomy using the official OMB manual as reference. Approximately 737 unique sponsors were classified. Each assignment was reviewed; approximately 40 ambiguous cases — defunct sponsors, small private companies, brands operating outside US business registries — were verified through direct research. Confidence levels are documented per sponsor in the public dataset.
The 2022 NAICS revision is used throughout, which is meaningful for one specific category: software publishers, which moved from code 511210 (pre-2022) to 513210 (current). Other 2022 changes — including the new codes for cloud infrastructure (518210), streaming services (516210), and social platforms — affect a smaller number of sponsors in the data.
