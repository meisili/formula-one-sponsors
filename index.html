The Money on the Grid

A 45-year data investigation into who pays for Formula 1

By Meisi Li ~8 min read

Every patch of carbon fiber has a price

On November 13, 2022, the Mercedes-AMG Petronas Formula 1 car rolled onto the grid at the São Paulo Grand Prix without the FTX logo that had been on it the week before. According to Law360 Bankruptcy Authority, two days earlier, the cryptocurrency exchange FTX had filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in Delaware. Removing the FTX branding did not cost Mercedes anything. The deal it represented, signed fourteen months earlier with a company then valued at $32 billion, was the kind of sponsorship Formula 1 had spent six years rebuilding around. A year later, FTX customers named Mercedes in a class-action lawsuit alleging the team had helped promote a fraudulent exchange.

This story is about who pays for that car.

Every patch of carbon fiber on a Formula 1 car has a price. A top team's annual operating costs can exceed $500 million — well above the FIA's $140 million budget cap, which excludes driver salaries, marketing, and engine programs. According to BlackBook Motorsport, sponsorship accounted for about 21.7 percent of Formula One's primary revenue in 2025, meaning most of the money comes from other sources, such as broadcasting and prize money. The names on the cars still serve as a record of which industries considered international motorsport visibility a worthwhile investment, and when.

For most of the sport's modern history, that ledger was dominated by a single category: tobacco. From the early 1980s through 2006, cigarette brands accounted for nearly a third of every title sponsorship in Formula 1. When the European Union banned tobacco advertising in motorsport in 2005, that revenue stream declined within two seasons — and what replaced it tells you something about how global money has moved over the last twenty years.

This project tracks 45 years of F1 sponsorship across two datasets: every title sponsor since 1981, and every visible sponsor on every car for the last six seasons.

What is a title sponsor? Throughout this story, title sponsors are the company in the team's official entry — "Stake F1 Team," "Oracle Red Bull Racing" — not every logo on the car. We use that definition for historical trend analysis going back to 1981, and the full sponsor roster for the recent grid.
Why does the data start in 1981? The first Concorde Agreement was signed in January 1981 at the Hôtel de la Concorde in Paris, ending a multi-year power struggle between the teams' association FOCA and the FIA's sporting arm FISA. The agreement formalized revenue sharing, governance, and team participation. It marked the start of F1's modern commercial era. Title sponsorship existed before — Gold Leaf at Lotus in 1968, Marlboro at McLaren from 1974 — but the framework around it wasn't stable until 1981.

Three acts in 45 years

The names that have appeared on F1 cars over 45 years fall into a surprisingly small number of industries — only ten of the twenty NAICS sectors appear at all. The data tells a story in three acts.

The tobacco era (1981–2006). Cigarette brands were the single largest category of F1 title sponsor for 25 consecutive years. Thirteen tobacco brands held title slots between them — Marlboro, Camel, Rothmans, John Player Special, Mild Seven, Gauloises, Gitanes, Lucky Strike, Benson & Hedges, West, Winfield, Barclay, and Skoal Bandit — sometimes multiple at the same time. Formula 1 has been at the center of debates over tobacco advertising, with a substantial number of teams being sponsored by tobacco brands in the late 1990s. This close association meant that both the sport and tobacco companies relied heavily on each other during that period.

The dependency went both ways. When the UK Labour government announced a planned ban on tobacco sponsorship after winning the 1997 election, Bernie Ecclestone, F1's commercial chief, donated £1 million to the party. Labour subsequently proposed that Formula 1 be granted a special exemption from the EU's tobacco advertising directive, a reversal that became known as "Berniegate". The donation was eventually returned amid public outcry. The EU directive that ultimately took effect in July 2005 made no exceptions for motor racing.

Title sponsorship at the time was effectively a tobacco market, one of the few channels where cigarette logos could still reach a global TV audience.

The vacuum (2007–2016). The collapse in tobacco title sponsorship was sharp: from three in 2006 to one in 2007. Ferrari was the holdout, continuing to display Marlboro-affiliated "barcode" graphics through 2010 wherever tobacco advertising remained legal. Public health researchers later argued the barcodes functioned as alibi advertising. Ferrari maintained they were part of the team livery.

Telecoms tried to fill the space — AT&T at Williams, Vodafone at McLaren, Orange at Arrows — and most exited within a few years. Industry diversity actually fell during this decade. The data shows seven NAICS sectors represented in the 1990s versus only five in the 2010s. Title sponsorship, as a structural feature of the sport, began to decline at the same time, even as overall F1 sponsorship revenue grew. Teams were increasing their sponsorship revenue by securing multiple commercial partnerships rather than relying on a single major contract, contributing to Formula 1's projected sponsorship investment surpassing $3 billion in 2026.

The reinvention (2017–2026). Liberty Media completed its acquisition of F1 on January 23, 2017, for $4.4 billion. The American media company brought a different theory of the sport: less aristocratic European tradition, more global entertainment property. Two years later, Drive to Survive premiered on Netflix. By 2022, F1 was averaging over 1 million US viewers per race for the first time, with 34% of its global audience under 24.

The post-Liberty era is the first sustained expansion of F1 sponsorship since the tobacco era ended. Three categories now share what tobacco used to hold: software, state-owned oil and gas, and crypto and fintech. Where tobacco was one industry with one motive, the current grid is many industries with overlapping ones. Aramco wants to be seen as a technology company, not just an oil company. Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund wants a foothold in Western luxury sport. Oracle wants its name next to Ferrari and Red Bull because enterprise software is now a brand business. Stake.com, banned from advertising in the UK, wants the legitimacy that comes with appearing in the same camera frame as Lewis Hamilton.

The decline is structural, not industry turnover. Total F1 revenue grew across this period. What changed is how teams package their commercial inventory. One large title contract became thirty or more smaller placements. Same or more money, distributed across more logos.

From European monopoly to multi-polar grid

The industries that have funded Formula 1 have shifted, but the geography has shifted further. For most of the sport's commercial history, F1 was structurally European — Italian tobacco brands, French oil companies, German auto suppliers, British banks. That pattern began to fracture in the early 2000s as Asian manufacturers entered, and broke open again in the 2020s when state-owned Middle Eastern energy companies arrived. Today's grid is multi-polar in a way it has never been before.

Western European dominance (1981–late 1990s). Italian tobacco brands like Marlboro and Mild Seven; French oil companies like Elf, Total, and Gitanes; German auto suppliers; British banks like Barclays and HSBC. Through the mid-1990s, Western Europe accounted for the overwhelming majority of title sponsorships on the grid.

Wave one — Asia Pacific (post-2000). Petronas joined Sauber as title sponsor in 2002. Panasonic became Toyota's from 2002 to 2009. Other Japanese names had carried title slots earlier — Canon at Williams from 1985 to 1992, Subaru briefly at Coloni in 1990 — but the 2000s established a sustained Asian presence.

Wave two — the Middle East (post-2018). Aramco joined Aston Martin as a commercial partner in 2022 and became sole title partner in 2024, in a deal that coincided with Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund increasing its stake in Aston Martin to 20.5%. Aramco called it a chance to deliver "best-in-class technology" through "advanced fuels and lubricants" — language designed to position a sovereign oil company as a technology partner.

Orlen, a Polish state energy company, joined Alfa Romeo in 2020. Across the same period, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Abu Dhabi, and Qatar all hosted races, and the sponsorship followed the calendar. As the sport added Gulf races, Gulf money flowed back into the teams.

Across all eleven teams, including Cadillac's debut season, there are 331 distinct sponsorship placements on cars and drivers' suits. That number has grown roughly 21% since 2020. McLaren alone carries 54 logos; Cadillac carries only nine.

What that saturation looks like, geographically, tells a story that Liberty Media has been telling shareholders for nine years. In 2020, 24% of full-grid sponsors were headquartered in the United States. By 2026, that figure is 41%, a 17-point swing in six years. Western Europe has dropped to 39%. The Middle East accounts for only 3% of sponsor count, but those few sponsors are concentrated almost entirely at the title or principal tier, where the money is.

US growth. The most consistent geographic shift across 2020–2026 is the rise of US-headquartered sponsors — from 24% to 41%, a 17-point swing in six years. That tracks directly with Liberty Media's stated strategic priority of expanding F1's American audience. Liberty paid $4.4 billion for the sport in 2017 with explicit shareholder commitments to growing US revenue. Drive to Survive launched on Netflix in 2019; the Miami Grand Prix debuted in 2022; Las Vegas followed in 2023. By the time Netflix released its fourth season, the show was the most-watched series in 33 countries during its release weeks. US sponsorship followed the audience.

Western European hold. Despite the US surge, Western Europe remains the second-largest source of sponsors at 39%. The category is broader than it looks — the data includes UK financial services, Italian luxury goods, German engineering, and French aerospace.

Asia Pacific flat. 8% in 2026, essentially unchanged from 2020. Notable given that F1 races in Japan, Singapore, China, and Australia.

The China gap. While the Chinese Grand Prix returned to Shanghai in 2024, Chinese corporate sponsorship has yet to match the strong local fan interest, with most support so far coming from Zhou Guanyu's personal sponsors rather than from major commercial partnerships.

About this project

This story grew out of a Bloomberg Graphics investigation into gambling sponsors on Premier League kits — which raised a question: what does the same analysis look like across forty-five years of Formula 1? Two datasets underpin the reporting. The title sponsor dataset covers every Formula 1 team entry from 1981 through 2026, classified by industry. The full sponsor dataset covers every visible sponsor on every car for the 2020 through 2026 seasons, sourced from team-released livery imagery, official team partner pages, and contemporaneous reporting.

Methodology & sources

Industry classifications use the 2022 North American Industry Classification System (NAICS), the same taxonomy used by the U.S. Census Bureau and federal labor data. Classifications were assigned using Claude (Anthropic), which mapped each sponsor's primary business activity to the appropriate NAICS code based on its training knowledge of each company. Around 40 ambiguous cases — defunct sponsors, small private companies, and brands without a US business registry presence — were verified through independent research, including LinkedIn company pages, corporate websites, and motorsport press archives. The author reviewed all assignments and takes responsibility for the final classifications.
