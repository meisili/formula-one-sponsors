§ 01 — Lede (corrected)

Every patch of carbon fiber on a Formula 1 car has a price. A top team's annual operating costs can exceed $500 million — well above the FIA's $140 million budget cap, which excludes driver salaries, marketing, and engine programs. Most of that money comes from sponsors, not broadcasting or prize money. The names on the cars are a public ledger of which industries decided global motorsport visibility was worth buying, and when.
For most of the sport's modern history, that ledger was dominated by a single category: tobacco. From the early 1980s through 2006, cigarette brands accounted for nearly a third of every title sponsorship in Formula 1. When the European Union banned tobacco advertising in motorsport in 2005, that revenue stream collapsed within two seasons — and what replaced it tells you something about how global money has moved over the last twenty years.
This project tracks 45 years of F1 sponsorship across two datasets: every title sponsor since 1981, and every visible sponsor on every car for the last six seasons.

§ 01 — Ferrari livery caption (corrected — and recommend switching to 1999 image)

Ferrari's 1999 livery was anchored by a single name above all others: Marlboro, which became Ferrari's title sponsor in 1997 in a relationship that lasted through 2010 in name — and effectively continued via the controversial "barcode" livery for several years after the EU tobacco ad ban took effect in July 2005. The 2026 SF-26 has no title sponsor at all: HP is one of 40 partners visible on the car, sharing space with IBM, Shell (still there after four decades), Ray-Ban, UniCredit, Santander, and a long tail of smaller logos. Where the 1999 car concentrated commercial attention, the 2026 car distributes it.

§ 01 — Why 1981 sidebar (corrected)

The first Concorde Agreement was signed in January 1981 at the Hôtel de la Concorde in Paris, ending a multi-year power struggle between the teams' association (FOCA) and the FIA's sporting arm (FISA). The agreement formalized revenue sharing, governance, and team participation requirements — marking the start of F1's modern commercial era. Title sponsorship existed before — Gold Leaf at Lotus in 1968, Marlboro at McLaren from 1974 — but 1981 is the first season after which the commercial framework was stable and continuously documented.

§ 02 — Three eras (corrected)

The tobacco era (1981–2006). Cigarette brands were the single largest category of F1 title sponsor for 25 consecutive years. Thirteen tobacco brands held title slots between them — Marlboro, Camel, Rothmans, John Player Special, Mild Seven, Gauloises, Gitanes, Lucky Strike, Benson & Hedges, West, Winfield, Barclay, and Skoal Bandit — sometimes multiple at the same time. In 1999, seven of the eleven teams on the grid had a tobacco brand in their team name — the peak concentration in the dataset. Title sponsorship in this era was effectively a tobacco market.
The vacuum (2007–2016). The European Union's tobacco advertising directive took effect in July 2005, and the FIA had separately agreed to phase out tobacco branding. The transition was largely complete by 2007 — though Ferrari continued displaying Marlboro-affiliated "barcode" branding through 2010. The collapse in title sponsorship was sharp: from three tobacco title sponsors in 2006 to one in 2007. Telecoms tried to fill the space — AT&T at Williams, Vodafone at McLaren, Orange at Arrows — and most exited within a few years. Industry diversity actually fell during this decade rather than rising; the data shows seven NAICS sectors represented in the 1990s versus only five in the 2010s.
The reinvention (2017–2026). The post-Liberty Media era — Liberty Media completed its acquisition of F1 on January 23, 2017 — is the first sustained expansion of F1 sponsorship since the tobacco era ended. Three categories now share what tobacco used to hold: software (Oracle, HP, Atlassian, Cognizant), oil and gas (Aramco, Petronas, Orlen) — much of it state-owned — and a wave of crypto and fintech (Visa Cash App, MoneyGram, Stake). Where tobacco was one industry buying global visibility, the current grid is many industries with overlapping motives.

§ 03 — Wave two corrected

Wave two — the Middle East (post-2018). Aramco joined Aston Martin as a commercial partner in 2022 and became sole title partner in 2024. Orlen — Polish, not Middle Eastern, but with similar state-energy logic — joined Alfa Romeo in 2020. Across the same period, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Abu Dhabi, and Qatar all hosted races, and sovereign-wealth-adjacent sponsorship followed the calendar.

§ 03 — Wave one corrected

Wave one — Asia Pacific (post-2000). Asian companies entered F1 commercially in waves. Petronas joined Sauber as title sponsor in 2002; Panasonic became Toyota's title sponsor from 2002 to 2009. Other Japanese names had carried title slots earlier — Canon at Williams from 1985 to 1992, Subaru briefly at Coloni in 1990 — but the 2000s established a sustained Asian presence.

§ 04 — Current grid (corrected)

331 Total sponsorship placements across all 11 teams, 2026 season
Across all eleven teams (including Cadillac's debut), there are 331 distinct sponsorship placements on cars and drivers' suits — counting title, principal, major, and minor partners. That number has grown roughly 21% since 2020. McLaren alone carries 54 logos; Cadillac, in its debut season, carries only nine.
What that saturation looks like geographically tells a story Liberty Media has been telling shareholders for nine years. In 2020, 24% of full-grid sponsors were headquartered in the United States. By 2026, that figure is 41% — a 17-point swing in six years. Western Europe has dropped to 39%. The Middle East accounts for only 3% of sponsor count — but those few sponsors are concentrated almost entirely at the title or principal tier, where the money is.

§ 04 — US growth bullet (corrected)

US growth. The most consistent geographic shift across 2020–2026 is the rise of US-headquartered sponsors — from 24% to 41%, a 17-point swing in six years. That tracks directly with Liberty Media's stated strategic priority of expanding F1's American audience and adding US races (Miami debuted in 2022, Las Vegas in 2023).

§ 05 — FTX section (corrected)

The story of cryptocurrency in Formula 1 fits into 36 months. In 2020, the grid carried two crypto brands. By the start of 2022, that number was 13 — across nine of the ten teams. On November 11, 2022, FTX filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. Mercedes suspended its partnership with FTX that same day and removed FTX branding from the cars at the São Paulo Grand Prix. The team — and Major League Baseball — were later named in a 2023 class-action lawsuit by FTX customers alleging they had helped promote a fraudulent exchange.
What's striking is what happened next. Crypto did not exit Formula 1. By 2026, there are nine active crypto sponsors on the grid — fewer than the 2022 peak of 13, but the names have almost completely turned over. FTX, Vauld, Velas, Iqoniq, QNTMPAY, and Floki are gone. OKX, Binance, Bybit, Kraken, Gate, and Tezos remain or have arrived. The category survived; the specific gamble each team made on it didn't.
